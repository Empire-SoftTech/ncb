Resume Action Verbs: 200+ Power Words That Do Real Work

A verb list is only useful if it changes what your bullets claim. Swapping “helped with” for “spearheaded” and stopping there just makes a vague sentence louder. This guide groups 200-plus verbs by what they actually prove, names the openers to retire, and shows the rewrites that matter.

Why the verb matters (and why it is not about sounding impressive)

The value of a strong verb is that it makes a vague bullet impossible to write.

"Was involved in the migration" can be true if you attended two meetings. "Migrated 40 services from EC2 to EKS" cannot — it commits you to a scope you either did or did not own. That is the actual function of a good verb: it forces specificity, and specificity is what makes a bullet worth reading.

Which is why swapping a weak verb for a grandiose one and changing nothing else is a waste of time. "Spearheaded involvement in cross-functional initiatives" is worse than what it replaced, because it now sounds like it is hiding something. The verb is step one; the object and the result are steps two and three.

The three-part bullet, once more

[past-tense verb] + [what you did it to, at what scale] + [what changed]. Every list below is only useful in service of that shape.

Openers to retire immediately

These are not weak because they are common. They are weak because none of them commits you to anything.

Retire thisWhyReach for
Responsible for…Describes an assignment, not an outcomeOwned, ran, led, managed
Duties included…Literally a job descriptionDelivered, handled, processed
Helped with…Unbounded contributionContributed X to, co-led, supported by doing X
Worked on…Says nothing about your roleBuilt, designed, tested, shipped
Assisted in…Reads junior even when you were notCoordinated, prepared, supported
Participated in…Attendance is not achievementContributed, represented, delivered
Tasked with…Passive; someone else is the actorTook on, owned, drove
Involved in…Maximum vaguenessName the specific action
Familiar with…Belongs in skills, and even there it is weakUsed X to do Y
Successfully…Filler — the result already proves itDelete the word, keep the result

One nuance on helped and assisted: sometimes they are the honest words. The fix is not to inflate your role but to name your specific contribution. "Contributed the authentication module to a platform serving 4M users" is both modest and strong.

Led and managed — authority and ownership

Use these when you actually held the remit. Overclaiming here is the easiest thing for a reference call to catch.

Led · Managed · Directed · Headed · Oversaw · Chaired · Coordinated · Supervised · Owned · Drove · Spearheaded · Orchestrated · Mobilised · Championed · Delegated · Prioritised · Governed · Steered · Convened · Marshalled · Ran · Piloted · Commissioned · Sponsored

In use
  • Led a team of 6 engineers through a 4-month platform migration, delivering with zero customer-facing downtime.
  • Chaired the weekly change advisory board across 3 business units, cutting failed changes from 11 to 2 per quarter.

Built and created — things that did not exist before

Built · Created · Designed · Developed · Engineered · Architected · Launched · Established · Founded · Introduced · Prototyped · Authored · Composed · Formulated · Devised · Assembled · Configured · Implemented · Deployed · Instrumented · Scaffolded · Drafted · Produced · Curated

In use
  • Built 14 REST endpoints for an order service handling 1.2M requests a day.
  • Established the first onboarding curriculum for the support team, used by every new hire since.

A caution on architected: it implies you made the system-level decisions. If you shipped one module inside someone else's design, "built" is the accurate word and it will not be questioned in a system design interview.

Improved and optimised — something existed and got better

Improved · Optimised · Streamlined · Refactored · Modernised · Redesigned · Rebuilt · Overhauled · Strengthened · Accelerated · Simplified · Consolidated · Standardised · Automated · Hardened · Tuned · Upgraded · Rationalised · Restructured · Enhanced · Stabilised

In use
  • Reduced CI runtime from 22 to 6 minutes by parallelising the test suite and caching dependencies.
  • Standardised handover on an SBAR sheet across a 22-bed unit, cutting missed-medication reports from 7 to 1 per quarter.

These verbs almost demand a before-and-after figure. If you cannot supply one, you probably want a "built" verb instead.

Analysed and researched — you produced an understanding

Analysed · Investigated · Diagnosed · Evaluated · Assessed · Audited · Benchmarked · Modelled · Forecast · Segmented · Quantified · Profiled · Mapped · Reconciled · Validated · Tested · Surveyed · Examined · Traced · Identified · Uncovered · Measured

In use
  • Segmented 900k customers by recency and category mix; the resulting three-tier campaign lifted repeat purchase rate 14% against a holdout.
  • Diagnosed a 3-month intermittent data mismatch to timezone handling in a nightly job, recovering 14,000 mis-stamped records.

The trap with analysis verbs is stopping at the analysis. "Analysed customer churn data" is an activity. Say what the business did differently as a result — that is the achievement.

Delivered and shipped — it reached the world

In use
  • Delivered a $4.2M ERP rollout across 6 plants and 3 countries, two weeks early and 4% under budget.
  • Migrated 2.1TB of transactional history to Snowflake with zero reconciliation variances at cutover.

Sold and grew — commercial outcomes

  • Secured €180,000 in early-career research funding as principal investigator.

The pattern that actually matters

Three rewrites, and note that in each case the verb is the smallest part of the improvement:

Before

Responsible for managing social media accounts and creating content for the company.

After

Grew a company LinkedIn following from 2,400 to 11,800 in a year by publishing three technical posts a week, generating 38 inbound demo requests.

Before

Helped improve the recruitment process and worked on employer branding.

After

Rebuilt the interview scorecard and structured the first-round format across 4 teams, cutting time-to-offer from 41 to 26 days.

Before

Participated in quality improvement initiatives on the unit.

After

Co-led the unit falls-reduction workgroup, introducing hourly rounding that cut the fall rate from 4.1 to 1.8 per 1,000 patient days.

In every pair, the verb changed and a scope and a result appeared. That is the whole technique.

Words that have lost their meaning

These are not forbidden, but they now carry so little information that they are usually worth the space you save by cutting them: synergised, leveraged, utilised, spearheaded, revolutionised, disrupted, transformed, empowered, facilitated, liaised, interfaced, actioned, ideated, operationalised, incentivised.

Three specific notes. Utilised is just "used" with extra syllables. Leveraged usually means "used" as well, and occasionally hides the fact that you did not do very much. Transformed and revolutionised set an expectation that a bullet almost never meets — if the change was genuinely that large, the numbers will say so without the adjective.

The same applies to the self-descriptions people put in summaries: passionate, dynamic, results-driven, detail-oriented, hardworking, motivated, team player, go-getter, self-starter, thought leader. Cut all of them and the page gets stronger. More on this in resume mistakes.

Tense, person and punctuation

  • Past tense for accomplishments, including in your current role — anything finished is past. Present tense only for genuinely ongoing responsibilities.
  • No pronouns. Start with the verb: "Built the reporting pipeline", not "I built the reporting pipeline".
  • No articles at the start. "Managed a team of 6", not "Managed the a team".
  • Consistent punctuation. Either every bullet ends with a full stop or none does. Mixed endings are the most visible sign of a document assembled in a hurry.
  • One or two lines per bullet. If it runs to four, it contains two achievements — split it or cut one.
  • Avoid consecutive repetition. Three bullets opening with "Managed" blur into one.

Bullet rewrite checklist

  • Every bullet opens with a past-tense verb, no pronoun
  • No bullet starts with responsible for, duties included, helped with or worked on
  • The verb honestly reflects your level of contribution
  • Each bullet names what you acted on and at what scale
  • At least half of your bullets end in a measurable result
  • No two consecutive bullets share an opening verb
  • Filler words (successfully, utilised, leveraged) removed
  • Tense consistent within each role
  • Bullet-ending punctuation consistent across the document
  • No bullet longer than two lines

Frequently asked questions

What are resume action verbs?

Past-tense verbs that open a bullet point and name a specific thing you did — built, migrated, negotiated, reduced, precepted, instrumented. They replace passive constructions like "was responsible for" and "duties included".

The reason they work is not that they sound impressive. It is that a specific verb forces a specific claim, which is what makes a bullet checkable.

How many different action verbs should one resume use?

As many as you have distinct kinds of contribution — usually 12 to 20 across a full resume. What you are avoiding is repetition: three bullets in a row starting with "managed" reads as one undifferentiated blur.

Do not chase variety for its own sake either. Reaching for a thesaurus verb you would never say out loud is more conspicuous than repeating a plain one.

Should resume bullets be past or present tense?

Past tense for everything you have finished, including earlier bullets in your current role. Present tense only for genuinely ongoing responsibilities in your current job.

Mixing tenses within a single role is the most common version of this error. Pick past tense for accomplishments and present for continuing duties, and stay consistent.

Are power words the same as keywords?

No, and conflating them is a common mistake. Keywords are the nouns recruiters search for — Kubernetes, IFRS 16, ACLS, Salesforce. Action verbs are how you frame the claim around them.

Verbs almost never appear in a recruiter's search query, so their value is entirely with the human reader. Both matter, for different reasons.

Can I start every bullet with a different verb?

You can, but it is not the goal. Clarity beats variety: if "built" is the accurate word twice in a role, use it twice rather than substituting "architected" for something you did not architect.

Avoid consecutive repetition, keep the verb honest about your actual level of contribution, and let the results carry the differentiation.

Do action verbs help with ATS screening?

Indirectly. Parsers index nouns and phrases, so verbs rarely affect whether you appear in a search. What they affect is the human decision after that — and the quantified result the verb forces you to attach.
